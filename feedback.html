Member Review

What Are the Issues
in Our Food System?

At our last meeting, members looked at data together and talked about what is working and what is not in Muskegon County's food system. We wrote up the key issues below. We need your help to make sure we got it right.

Read each section. Use the form at the bottom to tell us what we missed or got wrong. Takes about 15 minutes.
Distribution
The Issues
  • No central place to collect, store, and move food around the county
  • Hard to keep food cold during transport
  • Farmers and food buyers are not connected
  • Makeshift workarounds have filled the gap, but they do not scale
  • Rules and regulations make distribution harder
  • Which rules? We need to name them so we can understand and change them
  • People who need help do not know that others are working to solve this
Why This Matters
We grow food here, but it does not get to the people who need it. Farmers and buyers are not connected. There is no central place to collect and move food around the county.

Consumption
The Issues
  • Many people cannot easily get to food
  • Bus routes do not reach food sources in many parts of the county
  • Local food is not where people actually shop — not at Meijer, not at corner stores
  • CSAs require driving to farms outside the city, which many families cannot do
  • Food programs, markets, and agencies do not share information with each other
  • Food is too expensive for many families
  • Some people lack the tools or skills to cook at home
  • Teaching cooking and nutrition builds real demand for better food access
  • City and county planning does not prioritize food access
Why This Matters
People in Muskegon County go hungry even though food is available. Getting food depends too much on where you live and how you get around. The systems that could help are not working together.

Waste & Rescue
The Issues
  • Rescued food is routed out of the county before it can reach local pantries and food programs
  • Supply leaves Muskegon, then trickles back in through regional networks at reduced volume and quality
  • No local aggregation point or cold storage to receive, hold, and redistribute rescued food at scale
  • Lack of public will and incentive for waste reduction; limited community-level efforts
  • Composting infrastructure is largely absent — but composting could save cities money on beautification and become a revenue driver
  • Other cities have shown this works; worth studying those models
  • Redistribution seen as mitigation rather than economic or community opportunity
  • Regulation and liability barriers limit participation in food rescue
  • Point source reduction not addressed at the producer or retail level
  • Sociocultural practices and education gaps around food waste
Why This Matters
A lot of food that could be saved and shared here gets sent out of the county instead. There is no local place to collect and store rescued food. Pantries run out. Farmers lose food with nowhere to send it.

Production
The Issues
  • Land is too expensive and inaccessible for small and beginning farmers
  • County zoning is inconsistent from township to township — some townships exclude farming entirely
  • Small and mid-size producers are not connected to local buyers — schools, restaurants, and institutions do not know who is growing what
  • This is a producer-to-buyer gap, not a lack of alignment among farmers themselves
  • Farmers market vendor fees and requirements are not small-farm friendly
  • Secondary markets for food pay too little or do not exist locally
  • Seasonal and migrant workers fill a critical role in large farm operations — the issue is making sure that system works well for everyone
  • Small and mid-size farms are at risk of being bought out by large industrial operations
  • No shared system for collecting, storing, and moving crops to local buyers
Why This Matters
Muskegon County has good land and farmers who want to grow more. But land costs are rising, zoning rules vary wildly by township, and there is no clear path for a small farmer to find local buyers, get fair prices, or store and move their crops. The system is not built for them.

Processing
The Issues
  • Cold chain infrastructure gaps
  • Access to capital and entrepreneurial education
  • Food safety credentials and regulatory requirements
  • Heavily regulated at multiple levels of government
  • Labor-intensive work requiring specialized skills to reach viability
  • Difficulty aligning demand to efficient, profitable supply management
  • Disparity among minority-owned businesses
  • Limited access to certified commercial space
  • High demand for skilled labor with limited local supply
Why This Matters
Processing is where local food either makes it into the system or gets lost. It costs a lot, requires special certifications, and is heavily regulated. Small businesses and minority-owned operations face the biggest barriers.
